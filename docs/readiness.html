Build assessment · 4 September 2026 · gaps one and three addressed

What stands between NEST and the App Store

Four sessions in, NEST has a validated look, an unusually complete onboarding flow, and an art bible enforced in code. What it does not have is a game, a client, or a business. This is what I found reading the whole thing, with the numbers behind each call.

The honest verdict. The prototype is doing exactly the job a prototype should: it has settled the look, proved the pairing handshake, and killed the guesswork in the art direction. It cannot ship in any form, and nothing about making it shippable is a matter of polishing what is here.

Three separate gaps, and only one of them is more code like the code you already have. The other two are a platform decision and a compliance workload that most teams discover at submission, four weeks too late.

Gap one: it is not a game yet

This is the gap I would fix first, because it is cheap, it can be done inside the prototype, and until it is fixed there is no way to know whether the rest is worth building.

Fixed since this audit

The whole game could be finished in forty minutes

There is no cooldown, no daily cap and no rate limit anywhere in the codebase. A trivia duel pays 120 coins for about thirty seconds of tapping, and you can play it again immediately. Seventy nine rounds buys every item and every room.

This is fatal to the premise rather than merely unbalanced. The entire pitch is that the home records months of showing up. An economy you can exhaust in an afternoon records nothing.

Now capped. The ritual, the duel and memory each have a daily limit, the ceiling is 242 coins on a perfect day, and the catalogue grew to 49 props and six rooms worth 49,480 coins. Six months of engaged play earns about 36,000, which is every room and roughly 41 of the 49 props.

was catalogue 9,410 · duel uncapped · 79 rounds ≈ 40 minutes now catalogue 49,480 · ritual 1/day, duel 1/day, memory 2/day ceiling 242 coins a day, no way to exceed it

Played honestly, it used to run out on day 33

The curve now stretches to roughly six months for a couple who shows up daily, and the catalogue deliberately outlasts that so what you own is a choice.

Partly fixed, and I had this wrong

Every couple ends up with the same house

My first pass called this emptiness: 32 props filling 138 of 360 tiles. The arithmetic was right and the reading was wrong. Thirty eight percent footprint coverage is roughly what a genuinely furnished room looks like, so the house was never bare.

The real problem was sameness. With a catalogue anyone could afford entirely, every home on the street was identical, which quietly destroys the showcase. The fix is not more props to fill space, it is a catalogue bigger than what the curve buys. Now at 49 props with eight left unbought at six months. Honest assessment: eight is a start, and real variety wants two or three times this catalogue. That is content production, and it stays the long pole.

Missing

The public street is six fictional couples

The showcase is the second half of the product's promise and it is entirely seeded. There is no submission, no real ranking, no discovery, no way to find the people you actually know. Until the backend exists this cannot be tested at all, and it is the feature most likely to decide whether the app spreads.

Missing

No sound, at all

The art bible has an audio section: soft mallet instruments, felt piano, a small woody click on every placement. None of it exists. In a game whose entire appeal is tactile softness, silence is doing real damage to how the placement animation lands.

Gap two: it is not an app

NEST is a web page. The App Store takes a client build, and the art bible specifies Unity with URP. This is a rewrite of the rendering layer, not a port, and it is better to say so plainly than to discover it in month two.

What does not survive the move

  • Every prop, because they are procedural JavaScript geometry assembled from bevelBox, not modelled assets. They have to be built in Blender.
  • The toon material and the dome, which are hand written three.js shaders.
  • The whole rendering layer in src/diorama.js, 889 lines.
  • The storage layer, which is localStorage end to end.

What ports directly, and is worth more than it looks

  • The palette and the material discipline, already proven to hold across 32 assets.
  • The prop proportions, footprints and silhouettes, as modelling reference.
  • Every balance number, sitting in one BALANCE object.
  • The onboarding flow, screen by screen, including all the copy.
  • The endpoint shapes in src/api.js and the event list in src/analytics.js.
  • The section 16 checks, which should become a CI gate on the asset pipeline.
Missing

There is no backend, so two phones cannot pair

The two tab demo is real in the sense that two separate sessions genuinely negotiate a pairing, but they do it through shared browser storage. Nothing in the product works between two devices: not pairing, not the shared nest, not the street, not notifications. src/api.js describes the service that needs to exist, which is a real head start, but it is a description and not a service.

persistence localStorage only, 18 call sites realtime BroadcastChannel between tabs push, deep links, cross device sync none

Gap three: it would not pass review

At the time of the audit, two of these were automatic rejections. Both are now built, and the joint data question underneath them is decided and recorded, so the schema can be designed against something written down.

Fixed since this audit · Guideline 5.1.1(v)

Account deletion is now inside the app

Under the settings control on the dome. Immediate, irreversible, confirmed by typing the word. The person's name, birthday, identity and their own answers are erased; the nest they were in freezes rather than dying, because the other person built it too. Verified: the deleted row is nulled, the identity mapping is gone, the partner and the home are untouched.

POST /users/me/delete → user nulled, identity removed, nest frozen settings actions now → leave · delete account · nests you have left
Fixed since this audit · Guideline 1.2

The street now has what user generated content requires

A filter runs on every piece of text at publish time and rejects slurs, links, email addresses and phone numbers, saying which and why. Any home can be reported with a named reason, which hides it from the reporter immediately. Blocking is reversible. The contact is printed on the street itself. The fifth requirement, a person acting within a day, is a commitment rather than code; the queue they would work exists and reports land in it.

Honest caveat: the word list is a placeholder. Real moderation is a service that keeps up with how people evade one.

filter · report · block · contact → 4 of 4 in code 24h response → a rota, not a function
Decided, written down, implemented

Whose data is a shared nest?

Recorded in docs/joint-data-policy.md. A nest freezes: it is never inherited by whoever stays and never destroyed by whoever goes, because either of those lets one person act unilaterally against the other. Personal data is erased on deletion; joint creative output is not. The test for which side a field falls on is whether one person could have produced it alone. Freezing is one function, so leaving and deleting cannot drift apart.

Fixed since this audit

Leaving exists, and it is one neutral action

By decision, not a staged breakup: leaving is leaving whatever the reason, with a plain account of what will happen given a whole screen rather than a dialog. The nest freezes for both, both can still look at it from settings, and it never appears in the onboarding flow, so a previous relationship does not greet somebody starting again. Verified: the fork screen after leaving does not mention the old nest by name.

Missing

No business model

Nothing in the product is sold. If coins become purchasable that is in app purchase with all its rules and a serious rebalance, because a currency you can buy changes what the home records. If it becomes a subscription, the free tier has to still work for two people. Either way this is a design decision that reaches back into the economy, so it belongs alongside the balancing work rather than after it.

Already handled

Two things the specs got right in advance

Offering Sign in with Apple alongside Google satisfies the login services rule, and the sixteen year age gate is already enforced at both the details screen and nest creation, with no retry loop. Both would otherwise have been late discoveries. The store age rating will need to match the gate.

The order I would do it in

Sequenced by dependency rather than by size. Each step exists partly to stop the next one being built on a guess.

  1. Cap the economy and deepen the content Done

    Daily limits on all three games, a curve that runs about six months, two more rooms, 17 more props and a daily ritual that asks a real question instead of offering a tick box. The catalogue now outlasts the curve on purpose.

    What it revealed: a fully furnished six room home renders at 96,142 triangles against the bible's 100,000 lot budget. At this catalogue size the budget and the room count are in tension, and the real build will want instancing or another pass at the bevel segments.

  2. Decide the breakup, the deletion and the joint data policy Done

    Decided and recorded in docs/joint-data-policy.md, and implemented in the prototype: a nest freezes, personal data goes, joint output stays, leaving is one neutral action, deletion is immediate. Moderation for the street was built in the same pass since it touches the same surface.

    What the backend inherits: the freeze lives in one function, archived nests are reachable only from settings, and deletion has to reach backups, analytics and third parties, which the prototype cannot show.

  3. Pick the client platform and prove one scene on a real phone

    Unity is not necessary for what NEST is today. The 3D is 35 percent of the code and asks nothing of an engine: no shadows, no physics, no skinned animation, orthographic and static. What eventually forces Unity is the bible's own section 12, which makes two animated avatars required by version 1.0.

    The JavaScript route is not free either: react-three-fiber currently depends on an older expo-gl than the Expo SDK ships, which works in the web build and breaks on real devices. A WebView wrapper risks Guideline 4.2. Both long poles, asset production and proving retention, are engine independent, so the decision can wait and should.

    Why here: the answer changes the cost of everything after it, and the current frame rate numbers come from software rendering and are meaningless until measured on a real device.

  4. Build the service the endpoints already describe

    Auth, nests, memberships, invites, the realtime channel, push, and the street as a real shared surface with moderation built in from the first commit rather than bolted on before submission.

    Why here: everything two person is blocked on it, and the shapes are already written down.

  5. Produce the real assets against the checks

    Model the catalogue in Blender to the bible's budgets, and run the section 16 checks as a pipeline gate so the palette and silhouette rules hold across a catalogue several times the current size.

    Why here: it is the long pole, and it wants the platform decided first.

  6. Compliance, store assets and a closed beta

    Account deletion, moderation tooling, privacy labels and manifest, age rating, the monetisation decision implemented, then real couples in TestFlight for long enough to see whether the streak actually holds past week three.

    Why last: most of it depends on decisions taken in the steps above.

Rough shape, not a quote. Steps one and two are days. Step three is a week or two to a decision. Steps four to six are where a real team and a real budget go, and the honest range depends entirely on how much of the catalogue you want at launch and how you answer the monetisation question.

Everything above was measured against the working tree at commit 0fbee54, not estimated. The counts, the forty minutes and the day 33 come from reading the code and modelling the curve, and the two blocking findings come from grepping for implementations that are simply not there.
